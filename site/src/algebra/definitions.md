# Definitions and axioms

> **Note:** The mathematical definitions are optional background for using the library. If you prefer to skip the mathematics, go to [Installation and modules](../usage/installation.md).

## Element structure {#element-structure}

**Definition 1 (Element structure).** Each element set $E$ has a distinguished wildcard element $\\# \in E$ and a total order $\leq$. For a query element $p$ and a stored element $e$ in $E$, one-way matching is defined by

$$
p \trianglerighteq e \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (p=\\#)\lor(p=e).
$$

A stored $\\#$ indicates that the corresponding element set does not apply. A query for a specific firm therefore skips a term whose firm element is $\\#$. The wildcard is an ordinary member of the total order; matching does not define that order.

Definition 1 of the preprint gives a symmetric comparison. The query relation used here treats only a query-side $\\#$ as a pattern.

Usage: [Elements](elements.md).

## Bases {#bases}

**Definition 2.** A base is a tuple of elements, one from each element set. For element sets $E_1,\ldots,E_n$, the base space is $E_1\times\cdots\times E_n$. An account and a firm, for example, form a base with two elements. A query tuple matches a stored base exactly when each query element matches the corresponding stored element.

Here, “element” refers to a member of an element set that supplies a component of a base, while a “member of the redundant algebra” is a sum of terms.

Usage: [Bases](bases.md).

## Hat bases and terms {#hat-bases-and-postings}

**Definition 3.** A hat base pairs a base with one of two directions: `Not` (increase) or `Hat` (decrease). A query can select both directions with `HatNot`. Each term has a direction of `Not` or `Hat`.

A term pairs a hat base with a nonnegative real amount from the value domain $V\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$. We write $a:e$ for an increase and $a:\widehat{e}$ for a decrease at the base $e$. The redundant algebra is generated by its terms. In Haskell, `Posting` is the type that represents a term.

**Example 1 (Hat bases).** A base can consist of an account title alone, such as `Cash`, or pair an account title with a date or a name. For the base $e=\langle\mathrm{Cash},\text{2024-01-01}\rangle$, the hat bases are $e$ for `Not` and $\widehat{e}$ for `Hat`.

Usage: [Bases](bases.md) and [Amount types](redundant.md#amount-types).

## Redundant algebra and linear combinations {#redundant-algebra-and-linear-combinations}

**Definition 4.** A redundant algebra consists of finite collections of terms, with addition, scalar multiplication by nonnegative values, hat, bar, and a norm into the value domain.

Write $R$ for the redundant algebra and $V$ for its value domain. The five operations and the library's additional operation `compress` have the following types.

| Operation | Type |
|---|---|
| Hat | $R\to R$ |
| Addition $+$ | $R\times R\to R$ |
| Scalar multiplication $\ast$ | $V\times R\to R$ |
| Bar | $R\to R$ |
| Norm | $R\to V$ |
| `compress` | $R\to R$ |

Usage: [Operations](redundant.md#operations) and [Amount types](redundant.md#amount-types).

**Definition 5.** At each underlying base $e_i$, a member of the redundant algebra records two finite sequences of amounts. Write $f_i(e_i)=\langle A_i,B_i\rangle$, with $A_i$ the increase sequence and $B_i$ the decrease sequence. Two increases of 3 and 2 at the same base remain separate terms until an operation explicitly combines them. A member of the redundant algebra $x=\langle f_1,\ldots,f_n\rangle$ has finite support over the bases. An empty sequence pair contributes zero.

Let $a_{ij}$ and $b_{ik}$ be the amounts in $A_i$ and $B_i$. The sum records each amount as a separate term, including repeated terms at the same base.

$$
x=\sum_i\sum_j a_{ij}:e_i+\sum_i\sum_k b_{ik}:\widehat{e_i}.
$$

Usage: [Add the month's transactions](redundant.md#add-the-months-transactions).

## Operations {#operations}

**Definition 6.** The operations on the paired sequences are as follows.

| Operation | Effect |
|---|---|
| Hat | Swap the two sequences at each base, preserving every amount. |
| Bar | Total each sequence at each base and cancel the two totals. Keep the excess on the larger side and drop a zero remainder. |
| Sum | Concatenate the corresponding sequences. |
| Scalar multiplication | Multiply every amount by the same nonnegative scalar. |
| Norm | Sum all amounts on both sides over all bases. |

The library adds `compress`, which sums amounts separately for each base and each direction (`Hat` or `Not`).

Hat swaps the increase and decrease sequences of every component.

$$
\widehat{x}=\langle\widehat{f_1},\ldots,\widehat{f_n}\rangle,
\qquad
\widehat{f_i}(e_i)=\langle B_i,A_i\rangle.
$$

For bar, write $[z]\_+=\max(0,z)$. If $A_i=(a_{i1},\ldots,a_{ip_i})$ and $B_i=(b_{i1},\ldots,b_{iq_i})$, the remaining increase and decrease are

$$
D_i=\left[\sum_j a_{ij}-\sum_k b_{ik}\right]\_+,
\qquad
R_i=\left[\sum_k b_{ik}-\sum_j a_{ij}\right]\_+.
$$

Bar represents each nonzero remainder by a one-term sequence and each zero remainder by an empty sequence. With that convention, its components are

$$
\bar{x}=\langle\bar{f_1},\ldots,\bar{f_n}\rangle,
\qquad
\bar{f_i}(e_i)=\langle D_i,R_i\rangle.
$$

For example, two increases of 3 and 2 and a decrease of 4 on the same base have norm 9. Applying `compress` gives an increase of 5 and a decrease of 4. Bar leaves an increase of 1. In general, the contribution to the norm is

$$
\operatorname{norm}(f_i(e_i)) = \sum_{j=1}^{p_i} a_{ij} + \sum_{k=1}^{q_i} b_{ik}.
$$

Usage: [Select an operation](redundant.md#select-an-operation).

## Five axioms {#five-axioms}

Let $x=\langle f_1,\ldots,f_n\rangle$ and let $a$ be a nonnegative scalar.

1. **Hat is an involution.** Applying hat twice restores the original terms.

   $$
   \widehat{\widehat{f_i}}(e_i) = f_i(e_i).
   $$

2. **Scaling at one base.** The scalar acts on each amount in each sequence.

   $$
   a \ast f_i(e_i) = \langle a \ast A_i, a \ast B_i\rangle.
   $$

3. **Scaling across bases.** The scalar acts on every component.

   $$
   a \ast \langle f_1,\ldots,f_n\rangle = \langle a \ast f_1,\ldots,a \ast f_n\rangle.
   $$

4. **Norm homogeneity.** Scaling the terms scales their total.

   $$
   \operatorname{norm}(a \ast x) = a \cdot \operatorname{norm}(x), \qquad a \ge 0.
   $$

5. **Norm additivity.** Adding terms adds their totals.

   $$
   \operatorname{norm}(f_i(e_i)+g_i(e_i)) = \operatorname{norm}(f_i(e_i)) + \operatorname{norm}(g_i(e_i)).
   $$

The preprint lists the defining sum of the norm under the name “norm homogeneity.” The homogeneity law above follows from that sum and the scalar multiplication.

Axioms 4 and 5 hold exactly with exact decimal arithmetic when the amounts, intermediate sums, and products are representable. With binary floating-point arithmetic, summing stored amounts exactly and rounding once still permits the two sides to differ in the last digit: scaling can round individual products, and adding two norms can use two already rounded totals.

Usage: [Choose an amount type](redundant.md#choose-an-amount-type).

## Account classes and sides {#account-classes-and-sides}

**Definition 7.** An exchange base carries an account title with an account division and a home side. Its debit or credit side combines that home side with the direction. In the library, `whatPIMO` classifies the account as plus stock, input, minus stock, or output, and `fixedCurrent` classifies it as current, fixed, or other. Table 1 shows how the directions map to sides for ordinary accounts.

| Division | `Not` | `Hat` |
|---|---|---|
| Assets | Increase (debit) | Decrease (credit) |
| Liabilities, equity | Increase (credit) | Decrease (debit) |
| Revenue | Recognition (credit) | Reversal (debit) |
| Expenses | Recognition (debit) | Reversal (credit) |

*Table 1. The meaning of `Not` and `Hat` and the resulting side, by account division.*

This table also appears as Table 2 in [Record a withdrawal](../usage/withdrawal.md#base-and-hat-base).

For an exchange base $b$, `whatDiv` takes values in $\lbrace\mathrm{Assets},\mathrm{Equity},\mathrm{Liability},\mathrm{Cost},\mathrm{Revenue}\rbrace$. For ordinary accounts, `whatPIMO` is $\mathrm{PS}$ for $\mathrm{Assets}$, $\mathrm{MS}$ for $\mathrm{Equity}$ and $\mathrm{Liability}$, $\mathrm{IN}$ for $\mathrm{Revenue}$, and $\mathrm{OUT}$ for $\mathrm{Cost}$. When `isContra` holds, `whatPIMO` swaps $\mathrm{PS}\leftrightarrow\mathrm{MS}$ and $\mathrm{IN}\leftrightarrow\mathrm{OUT}$.

Let $\operatorname{opposite}$ exchange $\mathrm{Debit}$ and $\mathrm{Credit}$, and let $s=\operatorname{defaultSide}(\operatorname{whatDiv}(b))$. The division's default side is debit for `Assets` and `Cost` and credit for `Equity`, `Liability`, and `Revenue`. The home side and the side of a stored hat base are

$$
\operatorname{homeSide}(b)=
\begin{cases}
\operatorname{opposite}(s) & \text{if } \operatorname{isContra}(b), \\\\
s & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{whichSide}(\mathrm{Not},b)&=\operatorname{homeSide}(b),\\\\
\operatorname{whichSide}(\mathrm{Hat},b)&=\operatorname{opposite}(\operatorname{homeSide}(b)).
\end{aligned}
$$

Usage: [Base and hat base](../usage/withdrawal.md#base-and-hat-base) and [Classify an account and check its side](redundant.md#classify-an-account-and-check-its-side).

## Debit and credit decomposition {#debit-and-credit-decomposition}

**Definition 8.** An exchange algebra extends the redundant algebra with four decompositions. Each decomposition projects $x$ onto the terms whose hat bases $h$ satisfy its condition.

$$
\operatorname{dec}\_{\bullet}(x):=x\big\vert\_{\lbrace h\mid C\_{\bullet}(h)\rbrace}.
$$

| Decomposition | Condition |
|---|---|
| `decL` | $C_L(h)\iff\operatorname{whichSide}(h)=\mathrm{Debit}$ |
| `decR` | $C_R(h)\iff\operatorname{whichSide}(h)=\mathrm{Credit}$ |
| `decP` | $C_P(h)\iff\operatorname{hat}(h)=\mathrm{Hat}$ |
| `decM` | $C_M(h)\iff\operatorname{hat}(h)=\mathrm{Not}$ |

Deguchi and Nakano (1986, Definition 2.16) use left for debit (`decL`) and right for credit (`decR`). For example, a decrease in cash is a credit, and an increase in an ordinary expense is a debit. A sum of terms is balanced when its exact debit and credit totals agree.

Usage: [Split an entry into debits and credits](../usage/withdrawal.md#exchange-algebra) and [Read the debit and credit difference](redundant.md#decompose-and-check-the-record).

## Transfers {#transfers}

**Definition 9.** An accounting transfer table is an ordered list of rows written `from .-> to |% share`. The source and destination are bases without a direction. `Relabel` moves the entire exact balance at each matched source. `Portion n d` moves the fraction $n/d$ of that exact balance, with $0<n\le d$, and rounds the moved amount once.

A source wildcard matches any stored element. A destination wildcard retains the corresponding source element. The accounting table preserves written row order and permits a destination to match a later source. It rejects overlapping source patterns and destinations that match the source of the same or an earlier row. The algebra layer sorts its rules by source pattern.

The `transferEntries` function returns a cancellation term at the source and a term of the same amount at the destination for each amount moved. The destination direction preserves the debit or credit side of the balance being moved. Add these terms to the original ledger to record the transfer and retain the original terms. Apply bar explicitly to cancel opposite amounts at each base.

The preprint's transfer operation takes an arbitrary transformation $f:V\to V$. Version 0.7 removed the 0.6 algebra operations `MulBy` and `DivBy`.

**Example 2 (Unit conversion).** Let $x$ and $y$ record the following terms.

$$
\begin{aligned}
x&=1:\widehat{\langle\mathrm{Cash},\mathrm{Yen}\rangle}
   +1:\langle\mathrm{Products},\mathrm{Amount}\rangle,\\\\
y&=2:\langle\mathrm{Cash},\mathrm{Yen}\rangle
   +2:\widehat{\langle\mathrm{Deposits},\mathrm{Yen}\rangle}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Use a single `Relabel` row from $\langle\mathrm{Products},\mathrm{Amount}\rangle$ to $\langle\mathrm{Products},\mathrm{Yen}\rangle$. Adding the two terms returned by `transferEntries` to $x+y$ gives the transfer result $z$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
z={}&1:\widehat{\langle\mathrm{Cash},\mathrm{Yen}\rangle}
     +1:\langle\mathrm{Products},\mathrm{Amount}\rangle\\\\
   &+2:\langle\mathrm{Cash},\mathrm{Yen}\rangle
     +2:\widehat{\langle\mathrm{Deposits},\mathrm{Yen}\rangle}\\\\
   &+1:\widehat{\langle\mathrm{Products},\mathrm{Amount}\rangle}
     +1:\langle\mathrm{Products},\mathrm{Yen}\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$

Applying bar cancels the two $\langle\mathrm{Products},\mathrm{Amount}\rangle$ terms and leaves a net increase of 1 at $\langle\mathrm{Cash},\mathrm{Yen}\rangle$.

$$
\bar{z}=1:\langle\mathrm{Cash},\mathrm{Yen}\rangle
       +2:\widehat{\langle\mathrm{Deposits},\mathrm{Yen}\rangle}
       +1:\langle\mathrm{Products},\mathrm{Yen}\rangle.
$$

The preprint's example cancels only the pair at the source base $\langle\mathrm{Products},\mathrm{Amount}\rangle$ and retains the original terms at the other bases.

Usage: [Transfers](../exchange/transfer.md).

## Notes {#notes}

**Definition 10.** A note set $N$ is a totally ordered set with equality and a distinguished empty element $\mathrm{plank}\in N$. A note can contain a date, a description, or a transaction identifier.

Usage: [Declare a note type](../journal/journal.md#declare-a-note-type).

## Finite journals {#finite-journals}

**Definition 11.** A journal is a finite-support function $J:N\to E$, where $E$ is the exchange algebra. A note outside the support has value zero. Equivalently, the journal is the finite collection of its nonzero note-algebra pairs $(n,J(n))$. Equal notes identify the same algebra component.

Usage: [Attach notes to the month](../journal/journal.md#attach-notes-to-the-month).

## Journal operations {#journal-operations}

**Definition 12.** Hat and scalar multiplication act separately on each note's algebra, preserving its note. Addition combines the algebras at each equal note by redundant addition:

$$
(J_1+J_2)(n)=J_1(n)+J_2(n).
$$

Aggregation combines the note components under the empty note. Its algebra is $\sum_{n\in N}J(n)$; applying bar to that combined algebra gives the journal bar. The norm counts all amounts across notes:

$$
\operatorname{norm}(J)=\operatorname{norm}\left(\sum_{n\in N}J(n)\right).
$$

Journal projections take finite lists of note keys or hat-base patterns. The following signatures omit type parameters on `Journal` and class constraints.

- `projWithNote :: [n] -> Journal -> Journal`
- `projWithBase :: [b] -> Journal -> Journal`
- `projWithNoteBase :: [n] -> [b] -> Journal -> Journal`

`projWithNote` selects the listed notes by equality, and `plank` in a note list selects every note. `projWithBase` selects terms within each note using the one-way matching of Definition 1, with `HatNot` matching either direction. `projWithNoteBase` applies both restrictions, with an empty note list leaving all notes eligible. A base projection drops notes whose projected algebra is zero.

`transferEntriesBy :: (n -> n) -> TransferTable b -> Journal -> Either (TransferJournalError n b) Journal` maps each input note to a target note. For each target, it combines the algebras of the input notes that map there, applies the table to their combined ledger, and returns the additional terms under the target note. Add the returned journal to the original journal to record the transfers.

The preprint specifies the inputs to these projections as power sets of notes and bases.

Usage: [Select by note, base, and date](../journal/journal.md#select-by-note-base-and-date) and [Total amounts and combine notes](../journal/journal.md#total-amounts-and-combine-notes).

## Journal notation {#journal-notation}

**Definition 13.** Write $e\mid n$ for the journal that attaches note $n$ to the exchange algebra $e$. In Haskell, this is `e .| n` (`infix 5`). The operator binds more tightly than `.+`, so enclose a sum in parentheses to attach one note to the sum.

Terms with the same note belong to a single component of the journal. For example, $(e_1\mid n)+(e_2\mid n)=(e_1+e_2)\mid n$.

**Example 3 (Select transactions by date).** Let the notes $n_1$, $n_2$, and $n_3$ be the date strings `2024-01-01`, `2024-01-15`, and `2024-02-01`. A journal $J$ can record two sales and a purchase of products.

$$
\begin{aligned}
e_1&=100:\mathrm{Cash}+100:\mathrm{Sales},\\\\
e_2&=50:\widehat{\mathrm{Cash}}+50:\mathrm{Products},\\\\
e_3&=30:\mathrm{Cash}+30:\mathrm{Sales},\\\\
J&=(e_1\mid n_1)+(e_2\mid n_2)+(e_3\mid n_3).
\end{aligned}
$$

Pass the two January notes in the list supplied to `projWithNote`.

$$
\operatorname{projWithNote}([n_1,n_2],J)=(e_1\mid n_1)+(e_2\mid n_2).
$$

The preprint's date example describes the January notes with a predicate, while `projWithNote` takes the list of dates.

Definitions 1–13 draw on Appendix A of [*Accounting State Space as a Foundation for Economic Agent-Based Modeling: An Input–Output Benchmark and Inventory Extension*](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-8485050/v2), with clarifications in Definitions 1, 6, 9, 12, and 13.
